# What Did Ancient Humans Do When It Rained All Week? (draft 1)

Narrator: en-GB-RyanNeural. Target 2:30-2:45. Setting: a composite band of about twenty people, Ice Age Europe, ~20,000 years ago. The video says plainly that it is a reconstruction.

Tags: [E] evidence, [I] inference, [C] comparison with living hunter-gatherers. Each line is tagged so we can wrap [I] lines in "probably" and "archaeologists think".

## Script

**Cold open**
1. Twenty thousand years ago, somewhere in Ice Age Europe, it starts to rain.
2. Not a shower. A whole week of it.
3. No weather app. No hardware store. Just twenty people, and whatever they had on their backs. So what do you do?

*Title: What did ancient humans do when it rained all week?*

4. Nobody wrote it down. But they left a lot behind: hearths, tools, bones, even paintings. And a few hunter-gatherers alive today help us read it.

**Day 1: shelter**
5. Day one. First job: get out of the wet. Wet clothes steal body heat, and in the Ice Age that could kill you. [I]
6. A cave, if you were lucky. If not, a hut: a frame of branches, or even mammoth bones, covered in hides. In what is now Ukraine, people built exactly that. [E: Mezhyrich; hides are inferred from later Arctic huts]

**Day 2: fire**
7. Day two is about the fire. Even Neanderthals could strike sparks from pyrite and flint. But sparks and soaking wood don't mix, so the smart move was to never let the fire go out. [E: Sorensen 2018; "smart move" is I]
8. So the fire became a job. Someone fed it. Someone watched it. And every day, the stack of dry wood got a little smaller. [I]

**Day 3: food**
9. Then there's food. Hunting in the rain is miserable, so you eat what you saved. Meat dried and smoked over fire. One team argues people have been smoking meat for two million years. Not everyone agrees. [E/debated: Tel Aviv Univ. 2025]
10. Hunter-gatherers today, like the Hadza of Tanzania, lean on honey, tubers and fruit in the wet season. [C]
11. But by day six, the pile of dried meat was nearly gone. [I]

**Day 4: work**
12. Day four. A wet day is a work day. Skins to scrape. Tools to sharpen. Clothes to mend. Needles with an eye, just like yours, show up in Europe by about twenty-six thousand years ago. [E]

**Night: stories**
13. Then the sun goes down, and the fire changes the conversation.
14. The anthropologist Polly Wiessner recorded what the Ju/'hoansi of Botswana and Namibia talk about. By day, mostly complaints, gossip and trade. Only six percent of it was stories. By night, eighty-one percent. [C: Wiessner 2014, PNAS]
15. Stories about people far away. About spirits. About the time someone was chased by a lion. Stories that told a group who it was. [C]

**Day 6: art**
16. Deep in Lascaux, archaeologists found more than a hundred small stone lamps that burned animal fat. We can't say the paintings were made in the rain. But to paint down there, you needed time, and light. [E: lamps; "time" is I]

**Day 7: water**
17. By now the river is rising, and the camp climbs the hill. Living by the water was convenient, right up until it wasn't. [I]
18. One lake-shore camp in Israel, Ohalo II, was swallowed by the water. That's why its huts, hearths, and even its bedding survived twenty-three thousand years. [E]

**Close**
19. On the seventh day, the clouds break.
20. A week of rain was no disaster for them. It was a week indoors, with the fire, the stories, and each other. Which, if you think about it, sounds a lot like ours.

~345 words.

## Where the data lives in the picture (no charts)
- Day counter: the sun and moon sweep past; a tally of finger-marks grows on the wall.
- Dry wood: a stack beside the hearth that visibly shrinks, each log fed to the fire.
- Dried meat: a hanging rack, strips taken down each day, the last one gone on day six.
- Stories vs. gossip: speech bubbles. By day, small grumbles and trade icons. By night, the bubbles grow large and paint animals, spirits and a lion chase, drawn in the firelight.
- Rain: the river rises up the valley; the camp moves to higher ground.
- Lamps: a line of tiny flames across a cave wall, one per lamp.
